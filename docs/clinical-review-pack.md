## 1. Urgent pathways

from a permanently visible Get help tab. Every one follows the same order — what to do now,
then why it matters, then reassurance only where it is true. Please check the **action line**

### `movements` — My baby is moving less, or differently

**Routes to:** maternity-unit  ·  **Evidence type:** UK guidance

**ACTION LINE (shown first, largest):**

> Call your maternity unit now. Any hour, day or night.

**Explicit "do not" items:**

> - Don’t wait until morning.
> - Don’t try to make your baby move first — no cold drinks, no lying down, no sugary food.
> - Don’t use a home doppler. Hearing a heartbeat does not mean your baby is well, and it can falsely reassure you.

**Why it matters (shown after the action):**

> A change in your baby’s pattern can be the earliest sign that something needs checking. There is no target number of movements and no need to count kicks — what matters is that this feels different from your baby’s normal.

**Reassurance (shown last, only where true):**

> Most people who report one episode of reduced movements go on to have a straightforward pregnancy and a healthy baby. Checking early is exactly the right thing to do, and there is no limit on how many times you can go in.

## 4. Vaccination

### `vaccines-recommended` — The three recommended vaccines

**Evidence type:** UK guidance

**Text as shown to the reader:**

> Flu, whooping cough and RSV — all free, all safe, all needed every pregnancy.

> **Flu vaccine** — recommended at any stage, free on the NHS. Pregnancy weakens immune response, raising the risk of flu complications and hospital admission. Flu in pregnancy is also linked to premature birth, low birthweight and stillbirth. Protection passes to your baby for their first few months. Not a live virus — it can’t give you flu. Needed every year.

> **Whooping cough vaccine** — given around 20 weeks (from 16 weeks), ideally before 32 weeks for the best transfer of protection. Your antibodies cross the placenta and protect your baby until their own vaccination at 8 weeks. Not live. UK safety monitoring of around 20,000 women, published in the BMJ, found no evidence of risk. **Needed every pregnancy** — protection doesn’t carry over.

> **RSV vaccine** — offered around your 28-week appointment. Protects your baby against severe RSV, a common virus that’s usually mild but can cause serious lung infections in babies, for their first 6 months.

> **You can have more than one at once.** Don’t delay any of them just to combine appointments.

### `vaccines-live-travel` — Live vaccines, travel and malaria

**Evidence type:** UK guidance

**Text as shown to the reader:**

> Live vaccines are usually postponed. Malaria is genuinely serious in pregnancy.

> **Live vaccines** are usually put off until after the birth. These are MMR, BCG, oral typhoid and yellow fever. The risk is a theoretical one — no birth defects have been found. Now and then a live vaccine is still advised, when the infection itself is the bigger risk. That is a conversation with your midwife or GP.

> **Travel vaccines.** If you can, avoid travelling somewhere that needs one. If you cannot, talk it through with your GP. An untreated infection is often more dangerous to your baby than the vaccine is.

> **Malaria is serious in pregnancy** and can be fatal for both of you. Avoid affected areas if at all possible.

> If you have to travel, there are tablets you can take. Which one depends on where you are going, how far along you are, and your health history — so this needs a doctor, not a shelf. Avoiding bites matters just as much: use a repellent that is safe in pregnancy, cover your skin from dusk to dawn, and sleep under a net.

### `pain-relief-options` — Pain relief options

**Evidence type:** UK guidance
**⚠ Single source.**

**Text as shown to the reader:**

> TENS, gas and air, epidural, pethidine — what each does and its trade-offs.

> **TENS machine:** small electrical pulses through pads on your back, thought to boost natural endorphins. Drug-free and you stay in control — most useful in early labour rather than later stages.

> **Gas and air (Entonox):** breathed through a mask, works in 15–20 seconds. Won’t remove pain completely and may make you lightheaded or sick, but no harm to you or your baby, and it wears off fast.

> **Epidural:** local anaesthetic numbing the pain nerves, given by an anaesthetist (so not available everywhere). Works well for a difficult labour and unlikely to cause sickness — but can slow labour, and around 1 in 8 women still need additional pain relief.

> **Pethidine:** injection into thigh or bottom, takes around 20 minutes, helps you relax for 2–4 hours. Not suitable late in labour — it can affect your baby’s breathing and feeding. Can make you woozy, sick or forgetful.

> You can discuss preferences with your midwife and write them in your birth plan — and you can change your mind on the day.

### `antenatal-classes` — Antenatal classes & hypnobirthing

**Evidence type:** UK guidance

**Text as shown to the reader:**

> What classes cover, and what hypnobirthing does and doesn’t do.

> **Antenatal classes** usually start 8 to 10 weeks before your due date. They run weekly, for about 2 hours. They cover diet, exercise, labour, relaxation, ways of giving birth, caring for a newborn, and how you are feeling. NHS classes are free — ask your midwife. NCT run paid courses, including short ones for second babies.

> **Hypnobirthing** uses breathing, relaxation and visualisation. The aim is to make birth feel manageable rather than frightening.

> The benefits are real and measured. Women report more confidence and more control. They report less fear, less anxiety, and less pain. Some studies found shorter labours. It is also linked to a better experience of birth, whatever happens on the day, and it has no safety downsides.

> **What it does not do.** It does not change how many women have an epidural. It does not change how many births end in a caesarean. It cannot promise a birth without intervention.

### `postnatal-depression` — Postnatal depression

**Evidence type:** UK guidance

**Text as shown to the reader:**

> More than 1 in 10 women, within a year. Treatable, usually within 3–6 months.

> **Baby blues vs PND:** baby blues usually starts 2–3 days after birth and passes within about 2 weeks. Postnatal depression is sadness, hopelessness, guilt or self-blame lasting most of the time for weeks or months. It can start in pregnancy, soon after birth, or any time up to a year after.

> **More than 1 in 10 women** get PND within a year of giving birth. Fathers and partners can get it too.

> **Symptoms:** low mood that won’t lift · not enjoying things · trouble sleeping even when exhausted · difficulty concentrating · anxiety · avoiding people · guilt and self-blame · difficulty bonding with your baby · thoughts of suicide or of harming yourself or your baby.

### `postpartum-psychosis` — Postpartum psychosis — a medical emergency

**Evidence type:** UK guidance

**Text as shown to the reader:**

> Rare, sudden, and needs same-day help. Not the same as postnatal depression.

> Postpartum psychosis affects about **1 in every 500 mothers**. It usually starts suddenly, in the first two weeks after the birth.

> It is **a medical emergency**. It is not a worse form of postnatal depression. It is a different illness, and it needs help the same day.

> **Often the person it happens to does not know she is unwell.** That is part of the illness. It is why this is written for partners, friends and family as much as for mothers. > **Action on Postpartum Psychosis (APP)** runs a network of women who have been through it. Many people find that more useful than anything else in the early weeks.

### `expressing` — Expressing milk

**Evidence type:** UK guidance
**⚠ Single source.**

**Text as shown to the reader:**

> By hand or pump, and how long milk keeps.

> **By hand:** cup your breast in a C-shape 2–3cm back from the nipple, press and release rhythmically, rotating position as flow slows. No equipment needed beyond a sterile container.

> **By pump:** massage first to help let-down; start on the lowest setting; switch breasts as flow slows, then switch back. One breast often produces more than the other — normal.

> **Storage:** back of the fridge (not the door) up to 8 days at 4°C or colder · ice compartment up to 2 weeks · freezer up to 6 months at −18°C or colder. Label and date everything, and store in small quantities.

> **Defrosting:** best thawed in the fridge. If needed urgently, use warm water — **never a microwave**, which creates hot spots that can burn your baby’s mouth. Use immediately once defrosted; never refreeze. Warm with warm water only, use within an hour, and discard leftovers.

**Evidence type:** UK guidance
**⚠ Single source.**

**Text as shown to the reader:**

> Sore nipples, mastitis, thrush, colic, reflux — keep feeding through most of them.

> **Sore nipples** — usually from latch, not something to just endure. Get help early; let nipples air-dry after feeds; avoid soap; cotton non-underwired bras. Avoid nipple shields, as they don’t fix the underlying latch issue.

> **Mastitis** — swollen, hot, painful breast area, possibly with flu-like symptoms. **Keep feeding through it** — stopping makes the blockage worse, and it won’t harm your baby even with infection present. > **Thrush** — burning nipple pain, especially after feeds. Needs GP treatment (antifungal cream or tablets) — carry on breastfeeding through it.

### `breastfeeding-diet-meds` — Diet, medication and contraception while breastfeeding

**Evidence type:** UK guidance

**Text as shown to the reader:**

> No special diet. Most medicines are fine. Never bed-share after drinking.

> **Diet:** no special diet needed — varied and balanced is the guidance. Caffeine under 300mg/day (roughly 3 mugs of instant coffee). Fish: 2 portions a week, one oily; limit shark, swordfish and marlin to 1 portion a week. Peanuts are fine unless you’re allergic yourself.

### `making-formula` — Making up a feed — step by step

**Evidence type:** UK guidance
**⚠ Single source.**

**Text as shown to the reader:**

> Water must still be at 70°C+. Never add extra powder or water.

> 1. Boil at least 1 litre of fresh tap water (never pre-boiled water).

> 2. Let it cool **no more than 30 minutes** — it needs to stay at 70°C or above to kill bacteria.

> 3. Clean your surface and wash your hands.

> 4. Water goes in the bottle **first**, while still hot — check the level carefully.

> 5. Add powder using only the scoop that came with that formula, levelled off, not packed.

> 6. Secure the teat and ring tightly, shake to dissolve.

> 7. Cool under cold running water (lid on) before testing on your wrist — should feel warm or cool, never hot.

> 8. Discard any leftover made-up formula after the feed.

> **Never** add extra powder (risks constipation and dehydration) or extra water (under-nourishes). Never add sugar or cereal. **Never microwave** — uneven heating can burn your baby’s mouth.

### `formula-types` — Types of formula — what’s actually different

**Evidence type:** UK guidance
**⚠ Single source.**

**Text as shown to the reader:**

> Most babies only ever need first infant formula.

> **First infant formula:** whey-based, easiest to digest, the only formula most babies need for their whole first year. No evidence switching brands does any good or harm.

> **"Hungrier baby" formula:** casein-based, harder to digest — **no evidence it helps babies settle or sleep better.**

> **Goats’ milk formula:** nutritionally equivalent, but not suitable for cows’ milk allergy — the proteins are too similar.

> **Comfort formula:** partially broken-down protein, no proven benefit for colic or constipation; not suitable for diagnosed allergy.

> **Anti-reflux, lactose-free, hypoallergenic and soya formula:** medical supervision or GP prescription only. Don’t self-select these off the shelf.

> **Follow-on formula:** not before 6 months, and even then no proven benefit over continuing first formula to age 1. Labels look similar — read carefully.

> **Never give under 12 months:** condensed, evaporated or dried milk · goats’ or sheep’s milk as a drink · soya, rice, oat or almond "milks" · cows’ milk as a drink. All are fine in cooking.

### `types` — Types of loss, in plain terms

**Evidence type:** Charity guidance
**⚠ Single source.**

**Text as shown to the reader:**

> **Chemical pregnancy** — a very early loss, usually around or before 5 weeks, often before an ultrasound could detect anything. A positive test may turn negative days later, sometimes with a heavier-than-usual period. Rarely needs medical treatment.

> **Anembryonic pregnancy** (previously "blighted ovum") — a pregnancy sac forms but no embryo develops, usually found on an early scan. Often no symptoms until bleeding starts, since hormone levels can stay elevated.

> **Miscarriage** — loss before 24 completed weeks; the most common form. Usually due to a random chromosomal issue in the pregnancy, **not** caused by exercise, sex, stress, or anything you did.

> **Missed (silent) miscarriage** — the pregnancy has stopped but there’s no bleeding or pain; only found on a scan. Management options are waiting, medication, or a procedure — the right choice depends on your situation, and it’s your choice to make with your team.

> **Ectopic pregnancy** — the pregnancy implants outside the womb, usually in a fallopian tube. This is a genuine emergency risk — it cannot develop safely and can become life-threatening if the tube ruptures.

> **Molar pregnancy** — rare; the tissue that should form the placenta develops abnormally instead. Needs removal and hormone-level monitoring afterwards.

> **Second-trimester (late) miscarriage** — loss between 13 and 24 weeks. In UK law this is classed as miscarriage rather than stillbirth, even though that terminology can feel wrong for what families have experienced.

> **Recurrent miscarriage** — three or more losses. Investigations exist, but around half of couples don’t get a definite explanation even after full testing. That’s a real gap in medicine, not a sign something was missed.

### `assessment` — What happens at assessment

**Evidence type:** Charity guidance

## 12. Inequalities in maternity care

MBRRACE-UK figures paired with actions. Please check the figures are current and stated
with the right uncertainty — the published ratios move between annual reports.

### `picture` — The current picture

**Evidence type:** UK guidance

**Text as shown to the reader:**

> Maternal death — during pregnancy or in the year after — is rare in the UK, and the UK has one of the lowest maternal mortality rates in the world. That is the true context for everything below.

> Within that low overall rate, MBRRACE-UK — the body that reviews every maternal death in the UK — has consistently found the risk is not evenly spread:

> **Black women** are around 2 to 2.3 times more likely to die than White women. That gap has narrowed from roughly four times a few years ago, but it has not closed.

> **Asian women** are around 1.3 times more likely to die.

> **Women in the most deprived areas** are around 1.9 times more likely to die than women in the least deprived areas.

> The overall maternal death rate has risen slightly in recent years even as the ethnic gap has narrowed — so this is an active safety priority, not a solved problem.

### `why` — Why — what the evidence actually shows

**Evidence type:** Guidance + research

**Text as shown to the reader:**

> The obvious question is whether this is really about something else. Age, perhaps. Health conditions. Or deprivation. Researchers have tested exactly that, and the answer is no — **those things do not explain the gap**. One UK study found that Black women’s higher risk barely moved once age, health and deprivation were accounted for.

> **Getting care early.** Black women are around four times more likely to book late for antenatal care, or to miss visits. That matters, because early care is where screening happens and where problems get picked up.

> **Care that fits the person.** Reviews of maternal deaths found Black women were over-represented in cases where the care given had not been shaped around them.

> **Being listened to.** Studies and national surveys keep finding the same thing. Women from ethnic minority backgrounds are more likely to say their concerns were brushed aside, especially about pain.

> NHS leaders and government reports now name racism and bias as part of the cause. You are not imagining it. You are not being oversensitive if this has happened to you.

### `discharge` — Discharge

> Why: More discharge is normal and protective — it helps stop infection travelling up to your womb. It increases further toward the end.

> Helps: Healthy discharge is thin, clear or milky, without a strong smell. Loose cotton underwear, avoid perfumed products. A pink jelly-like "show" near the end can mean labour is starting.
