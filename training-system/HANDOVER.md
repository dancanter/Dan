### Why two start dates

They answer different questions and collapsing them is wrong in both directions.

- The **cut** is behaviour. From Saturday morning he is on the intake, the steps and
  the sessions, and every day counts toward the goals.
- The **weighing** is measurement. He refeeds the Friday night before, then spends two
  days shedding what it put on. Saturday and Sunday mornings are sodium and glycogen,
  not bodyweight. Seeding a ten-week curve on either builds every target off a number
  that was never real, and hands him a flattering first week that is pure water.

Starting everything Monday stops counting two days he is really cutting for.
Starting everything Saturday poisons the start line.

**Implement the weighing start as a rule, not a date:** the first Monday on or after
the cut start. A cut opening on a Monday weighs that morning. Pinning it to an
absolute date breaks the moment the cut start moves — every readout filtering on it
goes empty because the pinned Monday is in the future.

Every row of the tracking table judges a weekly average, so the line it is judged

average that reaches back across the start day blends a refeed into the number the

eating normally against a week of not. Both flatter the cut in its first fortnight,
which is exactly when he is most likely to act on the number.

**Do not delete the pre-cut weigh-ins.** Keep them in the log and in history; the cut
simply does not read them.

### The projection recalibrates under itself — watch for this
Because the curve is seeded from his own opening week, a cut that runs fast from day
one moves the target down underneath itself and reads as "on schedule" forever.
Comparing the **rate** against the taper catches it; comparing position against the
curve does not.

He has said twice that mornings where the scale does not move make him angry. **This
is the failure mode most likely to end the cut — not hunger, not the training.**

Answer it with his own numbers, not encouragement:
- How often the scale actually rose over his last fortnight (typically 40%+ of days)
- What the weekly average did across those same days
- How far apart his own new lows have actually been

Expecting a new low every morning is expecting something that has never happened, on a
cut that was working. Fat cannot arrive overnight: at his deficit, a real one-pound

|---|---|

| Saturday | **low — ceiling 4,600** |
| Sunday | **10,000** |
| Week | **89,600 = 12,800/day** |

Saturday is a **ceiling, not a floor**. A 15,000-step Saturday is not a better week, it

### Judge an effort by pace, not by what it was filed as
The same 8 km is a shakeout at 4:50/km and a quality session at 4:05. The file cannot
tell them apart.

- Threshold pace ≈ **88% of VDOT**
- **The effort line is 10% the slow side of threshold**
- At his current fitness that is threshold 3:53/km, line **4:16/km**

His own 8 kms sort cleanly: **4:05** the day he meant it, **4:39** a fun run with one
quick km in it, **4:46** and **5:09** genuinely easy.

Use a **ratio, not a fixed number of seconds** — as he gets fitter every one of those
paces moves together, and a flat margin drifts into catching easy runs by the end of
the block.

**Only judge when a pace was actually logged.** No pace, no opinion. A reclassified run
counts toward the hard runs and **not** toward the five-mile slot — he did not do an
easy five-miler that week, and the empty slot is information.

### Earned rest — only when ahead
He asked for this and drew the boundary himself: it fires **only when he is ahead**.
That restriction is the whole value. A rest prompt that appears every week is one he
scrolls past, and then it is missing on the week it matters.

Fire on either, after at least 10 days of cut, never in a deload week:
- **Weight ahead**: 0.4 lb under the week's target, **or** losing 0.15 lb/week faster
  than the taper asks
- **Sessions ahead**: a whole session clear of pro-rata on gym, hard runs *and* runs

**The rate threshold has to fit inside the safe band.** At 125 lb the taper asks ~0.73
lb/week and the band tops out at 1.00 — only a quarter pound of room. Set it too high
and every firing is a "too fast" warning wearing a rest prompt's clothes.

Message: take one or two days with no running and no gym, **keep the food exactly where
it is** — a rest day is not a day to eat less. Steps can stay. What it buys: glycogen
back, cortisol down, and the held water usually lets go within a few days of easing
off, which is why the scale often drops *after* a rest rather than during a hard week.

**If he is ahead because the rate is over the band, the advice inverts:** take the days,
but **eat more on them, carbs first**.

## 7. Study — it shares the recovery, that's why it's here

**SK297 and SK298** — two 30-credit modules, **8–10 hours each, so a 16–20 hour
week**. Term starts **28 September 2026**, four days after the cut's halfway point.

The point of tracking this is **not** to manage the degree. It is that **training load
and study load draw down the same recovery**, and an app that can only see the running
will tell him to push in a week he is already buried.

### Rules
- **Stay silent before term.** A bar reading "16 hours short" every morning for a
  fortnight is noise, and noise gets ignored right up until the week it matters. Before
  28 September it shows the countdown and nothing else
- Log hours per day; judge the **rolling 7-day total** against the 16–20 band
- Under 16: say how many hours short, and that **deep blocks beat scattered hours** —
  two more three-hour sessions close it faster than an hour a day
- Inside the band: say so
- At or over 20: *"That is a full week — protect the Saturday."*

### The combined-load warning
When **both** are high in the same week — three or more effort sessions, or two efforts
plus five gym, **and** 20+ study hours — say so explicitly:

> Neither on its own is a problem. Together, in a deficit, they are the combination
> that turns into a flat fortnight. **Drop the third effort session, not the study
> hours** — the deadline does not move and the session does.

That instruction direction matters. The training is the flexible thing; the coursework
deadline is not.

### How the Strava data actually gets in

**Correction to an earlier version of this document, which said "build the parser, do
not build the sync". That was wrong.** A published artifact *can* reach Strava — live,
on Dan's own page.

What is true: the page cannot call `strava.com` directly. CSP blocks every outbound
request, OAuth needs a redirect URI and a server, and the client secret would sit in
plain text in a file anyone with the link can read.

What the earlier version missed: it does not have to. A published artifact can declare
the **`mcp` runtime capability** and call **the viewer's own claude.ai Strava
connector** through `window.claude.mcp`. The call runs with Dan's credentials inside
the claude.ai shell — no secret in the page, no redirect URI, no server. The
declaration looks like this:

```json
{"mcp": {"servers": [{"server": "Strava",
  "tools": ["list_activities", "get_activity_performance"]}]}}
```

Two shapes in the page: a section that *displays* activities registers
`watchTool(server, tool, input, handler)` — it replays from cache, refreshes when
stale, and returns an unsubscribe to store; a one-off *action* calls `callTool` and
reads `result.payload`. Tool failures reject with `tool_error`; retry only errors
flagged `retryable`. Read the runtime's own `mcp.d.ts` type definitions before writing
any of it — they are authoritative over anything remembered.

Two caveats worth knowing. A page declaring `mcp` **cannot be shared publicly** — fine
here, this is Dan's private artifact. And the viewer is asked to allow the connector on
first use and can say no, so the page must still render and be useful with the Strava
section empty. Keep a paste-JSON import box as the fallback, not as the plan.

Everything in the tables above was pulled through an assistant with Strava access, so
it is correct as data whichever transport gets used.

He will, however, be **six pounds lighter**, worth roughly 2 seconds per mile per pound
— about **37 seconds off a 5 km from weight alone**.

- **Realistic end of cut:** 5 km around **17:20–17:30**, 400m around **57**
- **Sub-55 and sub-17:** on the reverse, when he is light *and* his glycogen is back
  and creatine is in

**The cut does not set the times. It sets up the times.** He arrives at 28 November
lean, then runs them in December and January when he is fuelled. That is the order
those two things actually happen in, and it is not softening the target.

## 10. Sleep — the top lever

He has named this himself as the most important thing in the cut, and he is right.

### The honest case
Not "it helps recovery". **At the same calorie deficit, short sleep changes what you
lose** — more of it comes off as muscle, less as fat. The studies are small; say so.
The direction replicates.

Three knock-ons specific to him: ghrelin up and leptin down makes holding 1,799
genuinely harder; cortisol stays elevated, which is the under-eye puffiness he already
worries about; and hard sessions land worse, which costs him the times.

**For the times specifically:** sleep extension has been shown to improve **sprint
times** in athletes — the best-known study put basketball players at ten hours in bed
and their sprints, reaction time and accuracy all improved. Small study, but the most
directly relevant finding there is to a 400m target.

**For his skin:** overnight is when barrier repair and collagen synthesis run hardest
and skin blood flow is highest. Chronically raised cortisol breaks collagen down.

### The phone
He has had a phone by the bed for years and is changing it.

**Most of the gain is not blue light, it is time.** People who move the phone out of
the room typically get **30–60 minutes more actual sleep**, fall asleep quicker, and
stop waking to check it. The first ninety minutes of the night are the deepest, so a
later bedtime cuts into the most valuable part, not the least.

The rule that works is **charge it in another room and use a separate alarm**, not
"don't look at it". Screens off 60–90 minutes before bed.

### Tracking
- **Hours** and a **phone-out** toggle
- **7.5 hours is the line** — not a bedtime, roughly where the evidence sits
- 7- and 28-night averages, a 14-night bar chart, and a **phone-free streak**
- The streak must not break because today is not ticked yet — a day that has not
  finished is not a failure
- Bands, not pass/fail: under 6 severely short, 6–7 short, 7–7.5 just under, 7.5–9 the
  range, over 9 long but not wrong
- **Unlogged nights must read as unknown, never as bad.** Reading a blank as a bad
  night fires fatigue warnings on evidence that does not exist. When three or more of
  the last seven are blank, say so, and say plainly they are being *ignored* rather
  than judged

## 12. Facial puffiness — ranked for him

1. **Sleep** — bigger than everything else combined. Short sleep raises cortisol,
   cortisol holds sodium, and the face shows it first
2. **Cortisol from the deficit itself** — sustained deficit plus low carbs raises
   cortisol, which has genuine mineralocorticoid activity. This is why the scale
   flatlines then drops all at once. **An argument for not dropping carbs below the
   floor**
3. **Salt — he has this backwards.** Acutely yes; chronically no. Kidneys adapt within
   days. A year on high salt has built no backlog. **Keep the once-a-day salt.** The
   lever is potassium
4. **Gluten** — specific to him. Accidental exposure causes inflammation and fluid
   retention. If he wakes puffy with no explanation, look back 24–48 hours
5. **Training load** — hard sessions + deficit + 12,800 steps is a lot of total stress
6. **Sleeping position** — on his back, head slightly elevated
7. **Alcohol** — worst single-night input there is
8. **Dehydration** — counterintuitive; under-drinking makes him hold more

**Morning puffiness is normal and resolves in 1–2 hours.** Judging his face at 7 am is
judging the worst version of it that exists, every day.

## 13. Implementation notes — bugs worth not repeating

These all actually happened.

**Week grids stored by position, not by date.** Moving the block start does not move
the sessions in it, it *re-dates* them — thirteen weeks of finished work silently
lands on days he has not trained yet. Detect with: **a session marked done on a day
that has not happened is impossible.** That single test catches every re-dated session
and can never catch a real one. Offer a one-tap clear that blanks only the impossible
rows.

**Clear every week key on reset, not just this block's.** The previous block ran to 13
weeks; clearing 1–10 left 11, 12 and 13 in storage waiting to be counted.

**Two counters describing the same window must share a source.** This repo produced
the same class of bug repeatedly: the goals panel and countdown disagreeing about days
(69 vs 68), two different week counts, a step target left stale in one place after
moving in another, a "10 weeks exactly" claim that was 9.86. **Derive, don't duplicate.**

**JavaScript `%` keeps the sign.** `-2 % 7` is `-2`, and `rows[-2]` is `undefined` —
the pre-block days vanished silently rather than failing loudly. Floor the division and
wrap the remainder.

**`+` binds tighter than `||`.** `n + arr[n % 10] || n + "th"` returns `"19undefined"`.

**Build dates day-first by hand.** `toLocaleDateString` renders "September 19" on a
US-locale browser next to a hand-built "28 November".

**Never let a function return a different shape on an early path.** `cutTally()`
returned without `through`, and `goalPace()` read it unconditionally — a throw waiting
for the day before the cut opened.

**Date-relative tests.** Any test hard-coding "71 days" goes red at midnight with
nothing wrong in the app. A suite that fails every night is one nobody reads.
